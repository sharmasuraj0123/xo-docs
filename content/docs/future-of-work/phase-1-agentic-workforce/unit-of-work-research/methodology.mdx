<Lead>Every claim in this note is testable. This page lays out the research program: what to measure, how to measure it, which prior methodologies to borrow, and what result would prove us wrong.</Lead>

The simulations in this note are existence proofs, not evidence. They show the
arithmetic works if the assumptions hold; they do not show the assumptions hold.
This page turns each claim into a research question with a study design behind
it, drawing on methods that already exist: state-comparison benchmarks from
agent evaluation, randomized controlled trials from field productivity research,
learning-curve econometrics from manufacturing, and telemetry standards from
observability.

## Phase 0: instrument before you experiment

None of the studies are possible without trustworthy measurement, so the first
investment is telemetry, not experiments. Every unit of work should emit a
standard trace: tokens in and out per step, tool calls, files touched, wall
time, and the before and after snapshots. The
[OpenTelemetry GenAI semantic conventions](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/semconv/gen-ai/)
already standardize token usage, model metadata, and agent-step spans, so the
unit-of-work record can be an extension of an existing standard rather than a
proprietary format. This matters for the research as much as the product: if
the meter is custom, every result is contestable; if the meter is a shared
standard, the numbers travel.

## Phase 1: build the benchmark (RQ1)

The model already exists. [τ-bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045) evaluates
customer-service agents by comparing the final database state against an
annotated goal state, exactly the snapshot-comparison rule this note proposes,
and its authors argue this trades slow human judgment for fast, objective
evaluation. A unit-of-work benchmark generalizes that design beyond databases:
a suite of realistic units (tickets, contract reviews, reports, code changes),
each shipped with a starting snapshot `S₀`, a weighted check set `G`, and a
budget `B`.

Three methodological requirements, each borrowed from documented failure modes
of agent benchmarks:

**Validate the checks against humans.** Hard checks (status fields, tests,
exact matches) are objective, but soft checks ("risks flagged accurately") need
an LLM judge, and LLM judges must be calibrated, not assumed. The protocol:
collect human gold labels on a sample of completed units, measure judge-human
agreement (Cohen's κ or Krippendorff's α), and require agreement comparable to
human inter-rater levels, roughly κ ≥ 0.7, before a soft check counts toward
`V(u)`. Recent work on
[judge-human agreement](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09738) and rubric-based
evaluation shows this is achievable but not free: judges drift, so agreement
must be re-measured on a schedule, and a jury of diverse judge models beats a
single judge.

**Measure reliability, not just success.** τ-bench's `pass^k` metric (the
probability that all k independent runs of the same unit succeed) is the right
shape for business work, where a process that succeeds 80% of the time is not
"80% good", it is unreliable. The benchmark should report `pass^k` per unit
type, since reliability is what businesses actually buy.

**Audit the benchmark itself.** A systematic review of agent benchmarks
([Establishing Best Practices for Rigorous Agentic Benchmarks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02825))
found that many contain exploitable shortcuts, broken verifiers, and gameable
checks that inflate or deflate scores. Every check in the suite needs an
adversarial pass: can an agent satisfy the check without doing the work? This
is the benchmark-level version of the environment-hardening argument in
[the environment page](/future-of-work/phase-1-agentic-workforce/unit-of-work-research/environment).

**Falsification:** if calibrated scorers cannot reach κ ≥ 0.7 with humans on a
majority of realistic unit types, the claim that units of work are objectively
verifiable fails, and the framework degrades into human review with extra
steps.

## Phase 2a: the verification audit (RQ2)

The [calculation page](/future-of-work/phase-1-agentic-workforce/unit-of-work-research/calculation)
simulated an 18% optimistic self-report rate. The real number is an empirical
question, and it is the single highest-value measurement in the program because
it prices the entire trust argument.

Design: run N units (target several hundred per task type) to completion. For
each, record three verdicts independently: the agent's self-report, the
snapshot scorer's `V(u)`, and a blinded human auditor's judgment as ground
truth. Report the confusion matrix for each verification source against the
human gold standard, with two headline numbers: the settled-but-incomplete rate
(paid for, not done) and the false-rejection rate (done, not paid). The second
matters as much as the first: a scorer that rejects good work taxes every unit
with rework and erodes trust from the agent side of the market.

Run the audit twice: once with cooperative agents and once under explicit
optimization pressure (agents prompted or trained to maximize settlement),
because the gap between those two conditions measures how gameable the checks
are before reward-hacking dynamics find the gaps in production.

**Falsification:** if snapshot verification's settled-but-incomplete rate is
not materially lower than self-report's, or if its false-rejection rate is high
enough that rework costs exceed the savings, environment-side verification
loses its economic justification.

## Phase 2b: the learning-curve study (RQ3)

The [business case](/future-of-work/phase-1-agentic-workforce/unit-of-work-research/business-case)
assumed intent cost decays as a power law. Manufacturing economics gives us the
exact tool to test this:
[Wright's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experience_curve_effects), under
which unit cost follows `Cₜ = C₁ · t^b`. The test is a log-log regression of
cost per unit against cumulative unit count: a straight line with negative
slope confirms a learning effect, the slope gives the learning rate (cost
multiplier per doubling, `2^b`), and the R² tells you how stable the effect is.

Design: a within-subject A/B. Take one queue of similar real units (a support
backlog is ideal: high volume, natural similarity). Randomize units between two
arms with the same model and same task distribution: one arm runs every unit in
a persistent environment where memory, records, and definitions of done
accumulate; the other runs each unit in a fresh context. Track the full token
trace per unit from Phase 0 instrumentation. Pre-register the predictions: the
fresh arm should fit a flat line (slope ≈ 0), the persistent arm a power law
with negative slope, and the persistent arm's asymptote estimates the true
execution floor `c_exec`.

Two confounds need explicit controls. Model drift: pin model versions for the
study window, because a silent model upgrade mid-study is indistinguishable
from learning. Task heterogeneity: stratify units by difficulty at intake (or
randomize order), because if easy units cluster late, the curve lies. The
memory literature gives reason to expect a real effect, persistent skill and
reflection memory measurably improves success rates and token efficiency in
[agentic memory systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12110), but the size and
the durability of the effect on business work is unmeasured. That is the
contribution.

**Falsification:** if the persistent arm shows no statistically distinguishable
negative slope, or the savings disappear once memory-retrieval overhead is
charged honestly, the cost-curve argument fails and the environment's value
rests on trust alone.

## Phase 3: the field RCT (RQ4)

Lab results do not settle whether the framework helps real businesses, and
there is a cautionary tale here:
[METR's 2025 randomized trial](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089) found that
experienced developers using AI tools were 19% *slower*, while believing they
were 20% faster, on the same tasks. Three lessons transfer directly.

**Never trust perceived productivity.** Every measurement in this program must
be telemetric or state-based. Participant estimates are recorded, because the
perception gap is itself a finding, but they are never the outcome variable.

**Randomize at the unit level.** METR randomized per task, not per person,
which controls for individual skill. The analogue: within each participating
team, individual work items are randomly assigned to be run as units of work
(definition of done, budget, snapshot verification) or as ordinary prompt-driven
work, with the same agents and models in both arms.

**Pre-register and hunt confounds.** METR evaluated 21 candidate explanations
for their result before publishing. The unit-of-work RCT should pre-register
its outcome metrics: verified outcomes per dollar, settled-but-incomplete rate,
intervention rate over time, and time-to-settlement, plus the analysis plan,
before data collection starts.

One more design caution from the benchmark literature: simulated users are not
a substitute for this study.
[LLM-simulated users are unreliable proxies](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17087)
for real ones in agentic evaluations, so RQ4 cannot be answered with synthetic
businesses. It needs real teams, real queues, and a commitment to publish
whichever way the result goes.

**Falsification:** if teams in the unit-of-work arm do not ship more verified
outcomes per dollar, or the overhead of defining and checking units eats the
gains for typical work item sizes, then the framework is a billing convenience
rather than an operating advantage, and the note's central thesis needs
revision.

## Threats to validity, stated up front

**Goodhart pressure on the checks.** The moment checks carry money, agents
optimize against them. The verification audit's adversarial arm measures this,
and check suites need versioning and rotation the way security rules do.

**Selection effects.** Units that are easy to specify are easy to verify;
early adopters will route exactly those units to agents. Every result must be
reported per unit type with the selection criteria documented, or the program
will overclaim generality.

**Novelty and learning effects in humans.** Operators get better at writing
definitions of done. That is genuinely part of the treatment, but it must be
measured (track specification time and revision counts per unit) rather than
left as a hidden cost.

**Vendor incentive.** XO benefits if the thesis is true, which is precisely why
the program leans on pre-registration, standard telemetry, a public benchmark,
and falsification criteria attached to every claim. The credibility of the
research is itself an instance of the note's argument: trust comes from the
environment the work runs in, not from the worker's self-report.
